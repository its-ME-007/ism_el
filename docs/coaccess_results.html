Co-access prefetch for tiered storage

When a file is read, which other files should move to the fast tier with it? Learning which files are read together beats guessing from the file's group, and a break-even admission rule removes about a quarter of the wasted moves.

Setup

  • 1,680 Wikipedia articles in 28 categories. Each category acts as an access-control group.
  • 8 days of hourly pageviews (2026-11-08 to 11-16), thinned to 54,878 reads. Day 1 is warm-up; days 2–8 (48,664 reads) are scored.
  • Three tiers with modelled device latency. The fast (HOT) tier is capped at 2–20% of total bytes.
  • Usage = total read latency + migration cost, in millions of device-ms. Lower is better.

Findings

  • Co-access + fallback is fastest at the 5%, 10% and 20% caps (2–5% faster than random) and ties random at 2%. It needs 22–33% fewer migrations than random.
  • Break-even admission keeps plain co-access latency and cuts its migrations by 21–31%. It has the lowest usage of every prefetch policy at every cap.
  • Under a cap, plain recency still has the lowest usage. With no cap (72 h idle timeout), prefetching wins: 0.38M against 0.44M.
  • Semantic (TF-IDF) neighbours did no better than random same-group neighbours, so semantics was dropped.
  • The confidence score is miscalibrated: admission needs about 0.67, but measured precision is about 0.34.

Limitations and next steps

  • One thinning seed, one dataset, one week. Latency is simulated, not measured on hardware.
  • Next: 3–5 seeds and 2–3 weeks with confidence intervals; calibrate the confidence score; count HOT-tier eviction churn in the break-even rule.
